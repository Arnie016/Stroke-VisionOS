# Procedure explainer: what families may see and hear

This file describes concepts for a clinician-controlled educational model. It is deliberately non-technical enough for family communication but not a substitute for a surgeon's patient-specific explanation.

## First distinguish the openings

| Term | What happens to the bone | Typical stroke-related purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Craniotomy** | A bone flap is temporarily removed and replaced at the end of the operation | Reach an aneurysm for clipping, remove a hematoma, control a bleeding point, resect an AVM. |
| **Craniectomy** | Bone is removed and deliberately left out | Give swollen brain space; the bone or an implant may be replaced later. |
| **Hemicraniectomy** | A large one-sided craniectomy | Decompress a swollen cerebral hemisphere. |
| **Suboccipital craniectomy** | Bone is removed at the lower back of the skull | Decompress a swollen cerebellum or evacuate a posterior-fossa hematoma. |
| **Cranioplasty** | The skull defect is reconstructed later using preserved bone or an implant | Protect the brain, restore contour, and sometimes improve neurological symptoms related to the skull defect. |
| **Burr hole / twist-drill opening** | A small hole rather than a broad exposure | Place an EVD or selected minimally invasive instruments; it is not equivalent to an open craniotomy. |

## Large decompressive hemicraniectomy

### Conceptual steps

1. The patient receives general anesthesia and physiological monitoring.
2. A large curved scalp incision exposes one side of the skull.
3. A broad bone flap is removed; an undersized opening can fail to relieve pressure and can constrict swollen tissue at the edges.
4. The dura is opened and expanded with a patch (duraplasty).
5. The scalp is closed without replacing the bone.
6. Intensive neurocritical care continues; the outward bulge is expected and monitored.
7. If recovery and swelling permit, a later cranioplasty reconstructs the skull.

### Purpose to animate

Show fixed skull → increasing swelling/shift → removal of a large bone segment and dural expansion → reduced constriction. Do **not** show dead tissue returning to normal or blood flow automatically being restored.

## Suboccipital decompression and cerebellar procedures

The patient is positioned to expose the lower back of the head. Bone over the posterior fossa is removed and the dura opened. Depending on the condition, the surgeon may remove infarcted, swollen cerebellar tissue or evacuate blood. An EVD may drain cerebrospinal fluid from a ventricle through a separate small cranial opening.

The model should show the fourth ventricle and brainstem so families understand why a comparatively small posterior-fossa lesion can be immediately dangerous. Do not imply EVD alone and decompression are interchangeable.

## Craniotomy for intracerebral hematoma evacuation

The surgeon creates a bone flap, opens the dura, reaches the clot through or around brain tissue, removes accessible blood, and secures visible bleeding. The bone is usually replaced unless swelling requires decompression. Key uncertainties are how much secondary pressure is relieved versus how much additional tissue must be traversed, and whether injury from the initial hemorrhage is already irreversible.

The visual should distinguish:

- superficial lobar versus deep basal-ganglia/thalamic hemorrhage;
- supratentorial versus cerebellar hemorrhage;
- open craniotomy versus a narrow minimally invasive corridor;
- clot removal from repair of injured brain (which the operation cannot provide).

## Microsurgical aneurysm clipping

A craniotomy exposes arteries at the brain's base. Under a microscope, the surgeon separates the aneurysm from nearby vessels and nerves, may temporarily control blood flow, and places a clip across the aneurysm neck while preserving parent and branch arteries. The clip normally remains permanently. If a hematoma is present, it may be removed through the same exposure.

The comparator view should show coiling as a catheter route from an artery to the aneurysm—not as an inferior or “less complete” version of surgery. Each route has anatomy-specific benefits and risks.

## Decompression for cerebral venous thrombosis

The cranial opening resembles hemicraniectomy for arterial infarction. Hemorrhagic venous infarct may be left in place or evacuated depending on mass effect and accessibility. Anticoagulation treats the underlying thrombosis and is often restarted after surgery when the team judges bleeding stability acceptable; the operation and anticoagulation solve different parts of the problem.

## Surgery after ruptured AVM

Use three separable layers:

1. **Emergency pressure control:** evacuate hematoma and/or decompress.
2. **Define the source:** catheter angiography and detailed vascular imaging.
3. **Definitive obliteration:** microsurgical resection, embolization, radiosurgery, or a staged combination.

Never animate removal of the hematoma as automatically curing the AVM.

## External ventricular drain

An EVD is a flexible catheter placed through a small skull opening into a ventricle. It drains cerebrospinal fluid and can measure intracranial pressure. It can become blocked, over-drain, bleed, or become infected. It treats hydrocephalus/pressure, not the original infarction, aneurysm, AVM, or most of the intraparenchymal clot.

## Shared material risks to discuss and visualize carefully

The treating surgeon must select the risks material to the actual patient. Common categories include:

- death or failure to prevent herniation;
- additional stroke or neurological injury, including weakness, language, vision, coordination, cognition, or seizures;
- recurrent or expanding bleeding;
- infection, meningitis, wound breakdown, or cerebrospinal-fluid leak;
- brain swelling, hydrocephalus, fluid collections, or need for an EVD/shunt;
- injury to arteries, veins, cranial nerves, or nearby brain;
- anesthesia, cardiac, respiratory, thrombotic, and positioning complications;
- need for ventilation, tracheostomy, feeding tube, rehabilitation, institutional care, or further surgery;
- skull defect protection and later cranioplasty; and
- cranioplasty infection, resorption, implant exposure, seizure, hematoma, hydrocephalus, or repeat reconstruction.

Do not present a generic risk list as the patient's individualized consent. Do not use a single pooled complication rate across different operations and populations.

## Postoperative path families should be prepared to see

1. Neurocritical-care monitoring, sedation, ventilation, repeat examinations, and repeat CT imaging.
2. A swollen face/scalp, dressings, drains, lines, and—after craniectomy—a temporarily soft or bulging skull defect.
3. Early uncertainty: sedation and evolving swelling can prevent reliable functional prediction.
4. Possible secondary procedures: EVD, shunt, tracheostomy, feeding access, re-operation, angiography, or cranioplasty.
5. Rehabilitation assessment for mobility, speech/language, swallowing, cognition, vision, and daily activities.
6. Repeated goals-of-care conversations as the trajectory becomes clearer.

## Visual safety rules

- Keep anatomical scale and laterality accurate; label educational simplifications.
- Offer a non-graphic mode and an explicit warning before blood, incision, bone removal, or tissue-shift visuals.
- Use a neutral “pre-operation / operation / possible postoperative paths” sequence—not a green success animation.
- Represent outcomes as branching possibilities, never a deterministic timeline.
- State that timing, incision, positioning, bone size, tissue route, drains, and reconstruction differ by patient and center.
- Disable unsupervised insertion of names, scans, measurements, or patient identifiers in the research prototype.
