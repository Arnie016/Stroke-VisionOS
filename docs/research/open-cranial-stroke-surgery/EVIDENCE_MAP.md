# Evidence map by clinical pathway

## 1. Malignant hemispheric ischemic infarction

### Clinical problem

A large middle cerebral artery, internal carotid, or combined-territory infarct can swell over the following hours and days. Because the skull cannot expand, pressure can shift and compress the remaining brain and brainstem. Decompressive hemicraniectomy removes a large section of skull and opens/expands the dura to give the swollen hemisphere room.

### What the evidence supports

- In carefully selected patients **60 years or younger**, surgery within 48 hours strongly reduces death and increases the chance of survival across modified Rankin Scale outcomes. DECIMAL, DESTINY, HAMLET, and their pooled analysis established this effect.
- In patients **older than 60**, DESTINY II showed a substantial survival benefit. Most additional survivors required help with daily activities; none in that trial achieved mRS 0–2 at six months. This is the clearest example of why mortality and disability must be presented separately.
- Evidence is much less certain after 48 hours, before deterioration in broadly defined populations, or in patients excluded from trials. Guideline-supported selection incorporates age, neurological decline, infarct size, timing, comorbidity, and patient values.
- Dominant-hemisphere involvement or aphasia should not automatically exclude surgery. Available comparative evidence does not establish worse global outcome solely because the dominant hemisphere is affected, but the consequences of language disability require explicit values discussion.

### What the operation does not do

It does not remove the arterial blockage, restore dead brain, or guarantee awakening, speech, walking, or independent life. The bone flap is usually stored and replaced later in a cranioplasty after swelling resolves.

### Family-facing core message

> “The stroke has already injured a large part of the brain, and that area is now swelling inside a fixed skull. The operation makes room for swelling and can prevent death from pressure. It cannot undo the stroke. The difficult tradeoff is a better chance of survival with a real possibility of major long-term disability.”

### Evidence certainty

High for early mortality benefit in selected younger adults; moderate for older-adult survival; low or very low for late surgery, many subgroups, individual disability prediction, and quality-of-life prediction.

## 2. Space-occupying cerebellar infarction

### Clinical problem

The cerebellum lies in a tight posterior-fossa compartment near the brainstem and fourth ventricle. Swelling can compress the brainstem or block cerebrospinal-fluid flow, causing obstructive hydrocephalus. Operations include suboccipital decompressive craniectomy, sometimes removal of infarcted tissue (necrosectomy), and sometimes an external ventricular drain (EVD).

### What the evidence supports

- Stroke guidelines recommend urgent neurosurgical assessment and decompression for neurological deterioration from brainstem compression despite maximal medical treatment.
- Ventricular drainage is recommended for obstructive hydrocephalus, but whether it is enough alone depends on infarct size, mass effect, neurological status, and the risk that draining cerebrospinal fluid without posterior-fossa decompression could worsen tissue shift.
- The evidence base is mainly retrospective case series and meta-analyses of heterogeneous observational studies. These suggest survival and meaningful recovery are possible, but cannot prove which operation is superior or define a universal trigger.
- New observational work compares decompression alone with necrosectomy and combined approaches; it is hypothesis-generating, not a basis for an autonomous recommendation.

### Family-facing core message

> “The stroke is in the small compartment at the back of the skull. Swelling there can press directly on the brainstem or block the normal fluid pathways. The proposed operation creates space at the back of the skull; a drain may also relieve trapped fluid. The aim is to prevent fatal pressure, but the exact outcome remains uncertain.”

### Evidence certainty

Guideline consensus for rescue is strong in clinical logic, but comparative effectiveness evidence is low or very low.

## 3. Spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage

### Clinical problem

Bleeding forms a clot within brain tissue. Injury comes from the initial bleed, disrupted tissue, mass effect, hydrocephalus, inflammation, and sometimes expansion. Open options include craniotomy to remove clot and control visible bleeding, decompressive craniectomy with or without evacuation, and posterior-fossa evacuation. EVD and minimally invasive evacuation are related alternatives.

### Supratentorial hemorrhage

- STICH and STICH II did not show a clear overall functional benefit from routine early open craniotomy compared with initial conservative treatment in their broad trial populations.
- Current AHA/ASA and ESO/EANS guidance therefore describes the usefulness of routine craniotomy as uncertain. It may be considered as a lifesaving measure when a patient is deteriorating, and selected superficial lobar hemorrhages may be more plausible candidates.
- SWITCH provides weak, inconclusive evidence that decompressive craniectomy plus best medical treatment may reduce death or very severe dependence in selected severe deep hemorrhage. The trial stopped early and substantial disability remained common in both groups.
- Minimally invasive approaches are increasingly relevant. ENRICH showed a 180-day utility-weighted functional benefit for early trans-sulcal evacuation overall, driven by lobar rather than basal-ganglia hemorrhage. MISTIE III did not improve its primary functional endpoint. These results cannot be generalized to conventional open surgery.

### Cerebellar hemorrhage

- This is a distinct emergency. Current AHA/ASA guidance recommends immediate surgical removal, with or without EVD, to reduce mortality when there is neurological deterioration, brainstem compression, hydrocephalus from ventricular obstruction, or a hematoma volume of at least 15 mL.
- Observational individual-participant evidence suggests surgery is associated with better survival in larger cerebellar hemorrhages but not clearly with better functional outcome. Smaller-volume surgery may not help and may harm; selection remains clinical and individualized.

### Intraventricular hemorrhage and EVD

- EVD treats obstructive hydrocephalus and permits intracranial pressure monitoring. CLEAR III found that adding intraventricular alteplase to routine EVD irrigation did not substantially improve good functional outcome, although mortality fell and the proportion of severely disabled survivors increased. Again, survival must not stand in for recovery.

### Family-facing core message

> “The blood clot is taking up space and has already injured nearby brain. Surgery can remove some blood or make room, and in a rapidly worsening situation it may be the only way to prevent death from pressure. For many deep or large brain hemorrhages, research has not shown that routine open removal reliably improves functional recovery.”

### Evidence certainty

Moderate evidence against routine indiscriminate supratentorial craniotomy; low certainty for lifesaving rescue and decompressive craniectomy; guideline-supported urgent cerebellar evacuation with observational outcome evidence; evolving evidence for minimally invasive techniques.

## 4. Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage

### Clinical problem

A ruptured aneurysm can bleed around the brain and sometimes into the brain itself. The aneurysm must be secured promptly to reduce rebleeding. Microsurgical clipping requires a craniotomy and places a clip across the aneurysm neck. Endovascular coiling treats from inside the artery and does not require an open cranium. Some patients also need open hematoma evacuation or decompression.

### What the evidence supports

- In aneurysms suitable for either treatment, ISAT found better one-year death/dependency outcomes with coiling; later evidence shows clipping has more durable complete occlusion and less retreatment in some settings.
- Current multidisciplinary guidelines favor endovascular treatment when both approaches are equally suitable for many good-grade anterior-circulation aneurysms, but anatomy, age, hematoma, branch vessels, center expertise, and long-term durability all matter.
- The 2026 ESO/EANS/ESMINT guideline supports urgent clipping combined with hematoma removal when a patient is worsening from a space-occupying intracerebral or subdural hematoma and open surgery is the needed route.
- Decompressive craniectomy after poor-grade aSAH is a rescue strategy. Observational reviews show that favorable recovery is possible, but do not prove benefit or identify the right patients.

### Family-facing core message

> “The immediate goal is to close the weak point in the artery so it cannot bleed again. A clip reaches it through an opening in the skull; coiling reaches it through the blood vessels. The team recommends one based on the aneurysm's shape and location, the blood already pressing on the brain, and which approach can secure it most safely and quickly here.”

### Evidence certainty

High for the coiling-versus-clipping comparison in aneurysms represented in randomized trials; much lower for rescue decompression and complex anatomy outside those trials.

## 5. Cerebral venous thrombosis with impending herniation

### Clinical problem

A clot in a cerebral vein or venous sinus can cause venous infarction, hemorrhage, swelling, and herniation. Anticoagulation remains foundational even when hemorrhage is present, but extreme mass effect may require decompressive craniectomy, sometimes with hematoma evacuation.

### What the evidence supports

- European and Canadian guidance recommends decompressive surgery for impending herniation as a lifesaving intervention.
- Evidence consists of prospective and retrospective cohorts, registries, and systematic reviews rather than randomized trials. These show that severe presentation does not preclude later independence and that meaningful recovery can occur over months.
- DECOMPRESS2 adds multicenter prospective information about disability, quality of life, patient/caregiver retrospective treatment preference, and postoperative anticoagulation, but cannot remove selection bias or define an individualized prognosis.

### Family-facing core message

> “A clot in the brain's drainage system has caused swelling and bleeding. Medicine treats the clot, but pressure is now threatening the brain. Removing part of the skull may prevent fatal pressure while the veins recover. Even very sick patients can improve over months, but we cannot know today how much recovery this person will have.”

### Evidence certainty

Very low for comparative effectiveness; strong expert consensus for rescue because untreated herniation is usually fatal.

## 6. Ruptured brain arteriovenous malformation

### Clinical problem

An arteriovenous malformation (AVM) is an abnormal direct connection between arteries and veins. Rupture may create a hematoma and mass effect. Emergency surgery may evacuate the clot and decompress the brain; definitive microsurgical removal of the AVM may occur during the same operation or later, depending on anatomy and stability. Embolization and radiosurgery are alternative or complementary treatments.

### What the evidence supports

- Professional guidance emphasizes individualized multidisciplinary assessment using hemorrhage pattern, AVM size and location, venous drainage, eloquence, associated aneurysms, age, stability, and team expertise.
- Evidence on immediate versus delayed definitive resection after rupture is mostly retrospective and confounded by severity and anatomy. Some low-grade superficial lesions can be safely removed early in expert centers; delayed treatment may allow swelling and clot planes to settle but leaves an interval rebleeding risk.
- The product must not generalize from unruptured-AVM trials to an acutely ruptured AVM or collapse hematoma evacuation, decompression, and definitive AVM cure into a single concept.

### Family-facing core message

> “The immediate problem is the blood and pressure. The abnormal vessel tangle is the source, but removing it now may or may not be safe. The team may remove the clot and pressure first, treat the malformation at the same operation, or secure it later after more imaging. Those are separate decisions.”

### Evidence certainty

Low or very low for acute timing comparisons; expert, anatomy-specific decision-making is essential.

## Cross-cutting outcome and equity cautions

- A modified Rankin Scale cutoff hides important differences: mRS 3, 4, and 5 represent very different levels of assistance, and aphasia/cognition may be underrepresented.
- Withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment can become a self-fulfilling prophecy in observational outcome data. Prognosis should be multimodal, repeatedly updated, and not based on one severity score.
- Communicate uncertainty explicitly. Early examination may be distorted by sedation, intubation, seizures, hydrocephalus, metabolic disturbance, or the evolving stroke itself.
- Disability is not synonymous with poor quality of life. Ask about the patient's own values; do not substitute clinician assumptions about life with disability.
- Provide a qualified interpreter at no cost when needed. Do not rely on children or untrained relatives to translate consent or prognosis except as a temporary emergency bridge allowed by local policy.
