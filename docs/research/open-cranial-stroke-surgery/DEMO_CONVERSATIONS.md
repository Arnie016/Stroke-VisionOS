# Synthetic conversation demos

> [!WARNING]
> These examples are fictional training material. They are not clinical scripts, do not include a complete consent discussion, and must not be used for a real patient without specialist and local legal review. The values, numbers, and recommendations are illustrative and must never auto-populate from a diagnosis.

## Demo 1 — malignant left-hemisphere infarction in a younger adult

**Situation:** A fictional 52-year-old with a large left MCA infarction is intubated, 20 hours from last known well, with increasing swelling. No numerical prediction is shown because important clinical details are still being clarified.

**Clinician:** “I’m afraid the stroke is very large. The left side of the brain is swelling inside a skull that cannot expand. If the pressure keeps rising, it can push on the brainstem and cause death.”

**[Visual: injured left hemisphere and midline shift; neutral colors; established injury remains visibly distinct.]**

**Clinician:** “This operation is called a decompressive hemicraniectomy. We remove a large piece of skull and open the covering of the brain to make space. We do not remove the whole stroke and cannot restore brain that is already injured.”

**Family:** “Will he wake up and talk?”

**Clinician:** “I cannot promise that. Surgery gives people like him a better chance to survive this swelling. Because the stroke is on the side usually responsible for language and has also affected movement, he may be unable to speak normally, may have weakness on the right, and may need help with daily care. Recovery can continue for months, but today we cannot know how far it will go.”

**Clinician:** “What has he said about living with major communication difficulty or needing physical help? What abilities matter most to him?”

**Teaching point:** Do not exclude solely for dominant-hemisphere stroke; make aphasia and dependence concrete and values-based. Do not show a “restored brain” after decompression.

## Demo 2 — older adult and the survival/disability tradeoff

**Situation:** A fictional 74-year-old with a malignant MCA infarction meets the broad clinical pattern represented in DESTINY II. The clinician chooses to show that trial after explaining patient-trial differences.

**Clinician:** “The operation's clearest benefit is reducing death from swelling. It does not usually return an older person with a stroke this large to independence.”

**[Evidence card selected by clinician: DESTINY II, selected adults 61 or older, surgery within 48 hours, six-month outcomes.]**

**Clinician:** “In that trial, about 33 out of 100 assigned surgery had died by six months, compared with about 70 out of 100 assigned medical care. But the survival came with disability: no participant was independent, and many survivors needed help with most daily activities. Your mother is not an average trial participant—her heart disease and her earlier level of independence may change what is possible.”

**Family:** “So you are saying she will be completely dependent?”

**Clinician:** “No. The trial gives us a range, not certainty for her. I am saying complete independence is unlikely, major help is likely if she survives, and there is still a chance she dies despite surgery. What did she say after caring for her sister about outcomes she would or would not accept?”

**Teaching point:** Same denominator and time horizon; mortality and disability shown together; no personal percentage; explicit trial mismatch.

## Demo 3 — cerebellar hemorrhage with hydrocephalus

**Situation:** A fictional 63-year-old is becoming less responsive with a 19 mL cerebellar hemorrhage, brainstem compression, and obstructive hydrocephalus.

**Clinician:** “The bleeding is in the tight space at the back of the skull. It is pressing on the brainstem and blocking the fluid pathways. Both can become fatal quickly.”

**[Visual: posterior fossa, fourth ventricle compression, enlarged upstream ventricles.]**

**Clinician:** “We recommend immediate surgery to remove accessible blood and relieve pressure. We may also place a fluid drain. The strongest reason is survival; research does not let me promise better functional recovery. Risks include more bleeding, injury to the cerebellum or brainstem, infection, fluid problems, need for ventilation or further surgery, disability, and death.”

**Family:** “Can the drain alone do it?”

**Clinician:** “The drain can relieve trapped fluid, but it does not remove the clot pressing on the brainstem. In this scan and with the worsening examination, our team believes the operation plus the drain is the safer way to address both problems.”

**Teaching point:** Cerebellar hemorrhage has a distinct guideline indication. EVD and evacuation have different purposes.

## Demo 4 — ruptured aneurysm: clipping versus coiling

**Situation:** A fictional patient has an aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage plus a large superficial hematoma causing deterioration. The vascular team believes open evacuation and clipping can address both.

**Clinician:** “There are two general ways to close a ruptured aneurysm. Coiling reaches it through the artery. Clipping reaches it through an opening in the skull. For many aneurysms suitable for either, coiling has better early recovery evidence.”

**[Visual: side-by-side catheter route and cranial route, equal visual status.]**

**Clinician:** “This case is different because the blood clot itself is pressing on the brain and needs to be removed urgently. Through the same opening, we can remove that clot and place a clip across the aneurysm neck. Our open and endovascular specialists reviewed it together and recommend clipping for that reason.”

**Family:** “Is clipping old-fashioned?”

**Clinician:** “No. They are different tools. Coiling is less invasive and is preferred in many suitable aneurysms. Clipping can be more durable and, in this case, provides the route to remove the dangerous blood at the same time.”

**Teaching point:** Evidence eligibility before statistics; multidisciplinary recommendation; do not frame the open route as technological failure.

## Demo 5 — cerebral venous thrombosis and uncertainty

**Situation:** A fictional 31-year-old with hemorrhagic venous infarction is herniating despite anticoagulation and neurocritical care.

**Clinician:** “A clot has blocked the veins that drain her brain. That has caused both bleeding and severe swelling. The blood thinner treats the clot; the operation makes room for the swollen brain. They treat different dangers.”

**Family:** “How can you operate and still give a blood thinner?”

**Clinician:** “That is a real balance. We control surgical bleeding and use scans, the examination, and the time since surgery to decide when anticoagulation is safe to continue or restart. There is no exact research-backed hour that fits everyone.”

**Clinician:** “Without pressure relief, we believe death from herniation is very likely. Case series show that even patients this sick can recover independence, sometimes over many months, but there is no randomized trial and I cannot give her a reliable personal percentage.”

**Teaching point:** Do not mistake low evidence certainty for absence of urgent clinical rationale; explicitly explain why randomized evidence is lacking and uncertainty remains.

## Demo 6 — teach-back and repair

**Clinician:** “To make sure I explained this clearly, please tell me what the operation is intended to do and what it cannot do.”

**Family:** “You will take out the damaged part and she should wake up once the pressure is gone.”

**Clinician:** “Thank you—that tells me I was not clear enough. We plan to remove part of the skull, not the whole damaged area. That may prevent fatal pressure. The stroke injury remains, so she may not wake normally and may have major disability. Could you tell me the difference once more in your words?”

**Teaching point:** Teach-back assesses the explanation. A wrong answer triggers clarification, never blame or a red failure score.

## Demo 7 — responding to “What would you do?”

**Family:** “Doctor, what would you do if this were your father?”

**Clinician:** “I can tell you what I recommend for him, and I want to base it on what mattered to him rather than on my own family's values. You told me he accepted physical dependence after his earlier stroke as long as he could recognize and communicate with family. Given that, and because the operation offers a meaningful chance of surviving the pressure crisis, I recommend surgery. I remain worried that this stroke may take away communication despite the operation.”

**Teaching point:** Give a professional recommendation linked to the patient's values; do not make the surrogate carry the decision alone.

## Demo 8 — when the recommendation is not to operate

**Situation:** A fictional patient has extensive established bilateral injury, progressive herniation, serious comorbidity, and a documented prior refusal of survival with profound dependence. The clinical team concludes the operation cannot meet the patient's goals.

**Clinician:** “We considered whether opening the skull could relieve pressure. Even if the operation technically created space, it cannot reverse the extensive injury on both sides. Based on the scan, examination, other illness, and his written wishes, I do not believe surgery can return an outcome he would accept. I recommend that we do not operate.”

**Clinician:** “That does not mean we stop caring for him. We will treat pain, breathlessness, seizures, and agitation, help you be with him, and explain every change. I am sorry—we hoped to have an operation that could help.”

**Teaching point:** A recommendation against surgery requires the same clarity, compassion, symptom plan, and non-abandonment as a recommendation for surgery.
