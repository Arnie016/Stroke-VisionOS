# Urgent family communication guide

**Purpose:** a clinician-facing structure for a high-stakes conversation when the patient may lack capacity and open cranial surgery is being considered. It is not a verbatim script, legal standard, or replacement for patient-specific consent.

## The 12-part workflow

### 0. Huddle before entering

In 60–90 seconds, align the stroke neurologist, neurosurgeon, intensivist, bedside nurse, and interpreter/social-work/chaplain support as available.

- What is known, unknown, and changing?
- What decision is actually needed now, by what time, and what can wait?
- Does the patient have decision-making capacity for this decision?
- Is there an advance directive, appointed proxy, documented preference, or prior conversation?
- Who is the legally appropriate decision-maker under local law? Who else should be present?
- What is the team's recommendation and why?
- Which outcome evidence reasonably matches this patient, and what important mismatch exists?
- Who will lead, answer operative questions, address emotion, document, and arrange follow-up?

Do not expose team disagreement for the first time in front of the family. If meaningful uncertainty or disagreement remains, name it honestly and arrange rapid senior review.

### 1. Establish people, language, and immediate needs

> “I’m Dr __, the neurosurgeon. Dr __ from the stroke team and __, your interpreter/nurse, are with us. Before we begin, please tell me your relationship to [name], how they prefer to be addressed, and whether anyone else should join by phone.”

Confirm preferred spoken and written language. Use a qualified medical interpreter. Ask whether the family can see/hear, whether they need seating, and whether a cultural or spiritual support person should be contacted.

### 2. Give a warning shot and one-sentence headline

> “I’m afraid the scan shows a very large stroke. The brain is swelling, and the pressure could become fatal.”

Pause. Use the anatomically correct version: swelling after infarction, a blood clot taking up space, blocked cerebrospinal fluid, a ruptured aneurysm at risk of rebleeding, or venous clot with herniation.

### 3. Ask what they understand

> “Before I show you the scan model, what have you been told so far?”

This reveals misunderstanding, conflicting messages, literacy needs, and the family's current emotional frame. Correct only the points needed for the immediate decision.

### 4. Explain what is happening now

Use no more than three concepts, then pause:

1. where the injury is;
2. what immediate danger it causes;
3. what is irreversible versus potentially modifiable.

> “This darker area is brain already injured by loss of blood flow. Over the next day or two it can swell. The skull is rigid, so the swelling can push the rest of the brain and the brainstem. We can make space; we cannot bring the already injured brain back.”

### 5. Name the decision and time pressure

> “The decision we need to make in the next hour is whether to remove a large piece of skull to make room for swelling. We have time to answer your questions, but waiting until the pupils change or the blood pressure collapses may remove the option.”

Use a real clinician-entered deadline or qualitative urgency; never a generic countdown. Separate decisions that can wait, such as later cranioplasty, long-term feeding access, or definitive AVM treatment in some cases.

### 6. Compare reasonable options

For each option, cover purpose, burdens, best case, most likely range, worst case, and what happens next.

| Option | Explain |
|---|---|
| Proposed open operation | What is opened/removed/replaced; mechanism; material risks; postoperative ICU path; later procedures; what it cannot repair. |
| Alternative procedure | E.g., coiling versus clipping, EVD versus/plus posterior-fossa decompression, minimally invasive versus open evacuation—only if genuinely suitable locally. |
| Active medical treatment without this operation | Specific ongoing treatment and the expected pressure/rebleeding risk. Never label this “doing nothing.” |
| Comfort-focused treatment | Relief of pain, breathlessness, agitation, seizures, secretions, and family distress when this matches the patient's goals; not abandonment. |
| Time-limited trial | When medically safe, define treatment, duration/event milestones, information to reassess, and actions at the review point. Do not use a “trial” to postpone a truly time-critical irreversible decision. |

### 7. Present outcomes without false precision

Use this order:

1. qualitative best/most likely/worst case;
2. the team's patient-specific clinical judgment and its uncertainty;
3. optional population numbers from a clinician-approved matched source.

Numeric rules:

- use natural frequencies (“33 out of 100”), not relative risk alone;
- use the same denominator for all options;
- state the time horizon (“at six months”);
- show both frames (“33 die and 67 survive”);
- show the full functional distribution when available, not only “favorable/unfavorable”;
- name the study population and key mismatch (“the trial enrolled people aged 61 or older, but generally excluded…”);
- use icon arrays only if accessible and tested;
- never calculate a personal chance by combining trial percentages, age, imaging volume, or a severity score.

Example:

> “In the DESTINY II trial of selected people over 60 with this type of very large stroke, about 33 in 100 assigned surgery had died by six months, compared with about 70 in 100 assigned medical treatment. No one in either group was independent by the trial’s strict definition; many survivors needed help with most daily needs. Those are group results, not a prediction for your mother. She differs from the trial because __.”

### 8. Respond to emotion before adding facts

Use NURSE/VALUE-compatible responses:

- **Name:** “I can see how shocking this is.”
- **Understand:** “Anyone hearing this with so little warning could feel overwhelmed.”
- **Respect:** “You are trying to represent what he would want while everything is changing.”
- **Support:** “We will stay with you and keep treating his symptoms whatever is decided.”
- **Explore:** “Tell me what worries you most about the operation—or about not operating.”

Silence is a clinical communication tool. Do not continue an animation while people are crying, dissociating, or asking for a pause.

### 9. Elicit values using substituted judgment

The surrogate's job is usually to represent the patient's wishes and values, not choose what the surrogate personally wants. Local law varies.

Ask:

> “What has [name] said about living with help for bathing, eating, communicating, or mobility?”
> “What abilities make life meaningful to them?”
> “What burdens—ventilation, feeding tube, months of rehabilitation, another operation—would they accept for a chance to recover?”
> “If they could hear us, what would they tell us to prioritize?”

Avoid assuming a disabled life is unacceptable. Avoid asking a family to select an mRS score as a proxy for values.

### 10. Make a values-linked recommendation

Shared decision-making does not require clinician neutrality when expertise and time matter.

> “Based on what you’ve told us—that she valued being able to recognize family but accepted help with physical care—and because surgery gives a meaningful chance of surviving this pressure crisis, our recommendation is to operate. I am worried she may remain unable to speak or live independently.”

Or:

> “Because he consistently said he would not accept survival dependent on others for all bodily care, and because surgery is very unlikely to return the abilities he valued, I recommend we do not perform the decompression and instead focus treatment on comfort and time with family.”

The recommendation must come from the responsible clinical team, never from software.

### 11. Teach back, decide, document, and plan the next meeting

> “I know this is a lot. To make sure I explained it clearly, can you tell me in your own words what the operation is meant to do, what it cannot do, and the main tradeoff?”

Repair misunderstandings, then invite questions. Document:

- capacity and decision-maker authority;
- participants and interpreter name/ID;
- diagnosis, uncertainty, and urgency;
- proposed operation and reasonable alternatives;
- material benefits, burdens, and patient-specific risks discussed;
- population numbers/source shown, if any;
- known patient wishes and values;
- clinician recommendation and rationale;
- family's teach-back and questions;
- consent/refusal/emergency basis under local process;
- what remains uncertain; and
- next update time and responsible clinician.

## Phrases to avoid and safer alternatives

| Avoid | Why | Prefer |
|---|---|---|
| “The surgery will save them.” | Promises an outcome; ignores intraoperative and postoperative death. | “The surgery may prevent death from pressure, but we cannot guarantee survival.” |
| “Successful surgery.” | Technical success and patient-valued recovery are different. | “We may successfully relieve pressure and still see severe disability from the original stroke.” |
| “Do you want us to do everything?” | Undefined, coercive, and hides tradeoffs. | “The decision today is whether to __. Let me compare what each option could mean.” |
| “There is nothing more we can do.” | Abandoning; symptom and family care always continue. | “We cannot reverse the injury, but we can treat discomfort and support your family.” |
| “They will be a vegetable / have no quality of life.” | Dehumanizing and usually unjustifiably certain. | Describe specific abilities, assistance, uncertainty, and the patient's own values. |
| “The score says…” | Scores do not decide individual treatment or values. | “The score describes severity in groups; our judgment also uses the exam, scans, trend, treatment response, and what matters to them.” |
| “No surgery means no treatment.” | False. | List active medical and comfort treatment explicitly. |
| “Sign here so we can start.” | Reduces consent to a transaction. | “Before we document the decision, what questions remain, and can you tell me what you understand?” |
| “The family consented” when the patient lacks capacity | Family legal authority and role vary. | Record the actual legal/clinical basis and the patient's represented wishes. |

## Visual-session rules

- Ask permission before showing graphic or immersive material.
- Start with orientation: top/bottom, left/right, and what is simplified.
- Limit each view to one message; hide nonessential controls.
- Keep the clinician in control of pace, layers, evidence, and stopping.
- Provide seated, 2D, low-motion, non-graphic, captioned, high-contrast, and interpreter-compatible modes.
- Never isolate a distressed relative in a headset. Maintain audible contact and an easy exit.
- End on the decision and next step, not on a dramatic animation.

## Emergency-consent legal warning

Capacity, advance directives, substitute decision-making, assent, documentation, and treatment without consent are jurisdiction-specific. The application must load an institution-approved local policy module and show its effective date. Generic wording in this repository must never be used to determine who has authority or whether emergency treatment can proceed.
